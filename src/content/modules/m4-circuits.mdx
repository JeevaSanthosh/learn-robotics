Everything your simulated robot does — the LED, the motors, the sensor — runs on electricity in the real world. Three words unlock all of it.

A <Term id="circuit">circuit</Term> is a complete loop that electricity can flow around. Break the loop anywhere and *everything* stops — that's literally what a switch does: it opens and closes the loop.

<Term id="current">Current</Term> is the flow itself — how much electricity is moving. Think water in a hose.

<Term id="voltage">Voltage</Term> is the *push* behind the flow — like water pressure. A 9V battery pushes harder than a 1.5V one.

So an <Term id="led">LED</Term> lighting up means: a battery pushes (voltage) a flow (current) around a complete loop (circuit) and through the LED, which turns some of that flow into light. That's it. That's electronics.

One more part is hiding in your robot: the <Term id="microcontroller">microcontroller</Term> — a tiny cheap computer whose job is to open and close many little circuits very fast. When your program says *turn LED on*, the microcontroller closes the LED's loop. Your blocks have been operating switches all along.

## Under the hood: the one equation

You just moved two sliders and watched a number change. That number came from one equation. The third piece is <Term id="resistance">resistance</Term> — how hard the path fights back, measured in ohms (Ω). All three lock together in <Term id="ohms-law">Ohm's law</Term>:

```
current = voltage ÷ resistance        I = V ÷ R
```

That is the whole thing. Push harder (more volts) and more flows. Fight harder (more ohms) and less flows. Scroll back up and check the demo against it — the numbers are the same numbers.

**Why you should care right now:** an LED will happily draw far more current than it can survive. So you put a resistor in series to hold the current down — that is what the little resistor next to every LED in every kit is doing. It is not decoration; it is Ohm's law keeping your LED alive.

A quick way to sanity-check any circuit you build later:

| If you… | Then current… | Because |
|---|---|---|
| swap 9V for 3V | drops to a third | less push, same fight |
| swap 470Ω for 220Ω | roughly doubles | less fight, same push |
| forget the resistor | shoots up | almost nothing fighting it — 💥 |

Go back to the demo and confirm each row of that table with the sliders. If a row doesn't match what you see, trust the demo — it's running the same equation the real circuit will.
