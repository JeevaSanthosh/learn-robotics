A <Term id="motor">motor</Term> spins fast but weakly. A robot wheel needs the opposite: slower, stronger turning. The fix is one of the oldest tricks in engineering: <Term id="gear">gears</Term>.

Mesh a small gear into a big one and you *trade*: the big gear turns slower but with more twisting strength — more <Term id="torque">torque</Term>. It's exactly a bike's low gear: your legs spin easily, the wheel turns with force, and you climb the hill. Speed and strength are a slider, not a menu — more of one is always less of the other.

Your simulator has that slider too. The **set speed** block uses a <Term id="variable">variable</Term> — a named box holding a number the program can read and change.

## Under the hood: gear ratios are just division

"Slower but stronger" is the feel. The number behind it is the <Term id="gear-ratio">gear ratio</Term>, and you get it by dividing teeth:

```
ratio = teeth on driven gear ÷ teeth on driving gear
```

A 10-tooth motor gear turning a 40-tooth wheel gear is 40 ÷ 10 = **4:1**. The wheel turns **4× slower** and with roughly **4× more torque**. Reverse them and you get 1:4 — four times faster, a quarter the strength.

There is no free lunch here, and the reason is <Term id="power">power</Term>:

```
power ≈ torque × speed
```

Gears slide the balance between torque and speed. They never increase the product. If a robot can't climb a ramp, gearing down 4:1 will get it up — and it will also crawl on the flat afterwards. That trade is the entire job of a drivetrain designer.

**Working it out for a real robot:** say your motor spins 200 rpm and you want the wheel at 50 rpm. You need 200 ÷ 50 = 4:1, so pair a 10-tooth with a 40-tooth. You've just done drivetrain design.

## Under the hood: how "set speed" actually works

A motor has no volume knob. It is on or off. So how does *set speed to 2* mean anything?

The microcontroller switches the motor on and off **very fast** — thousands of times a second — and varies how much of each cycle is "on". That fraction is called the duty cycle, and the technique is <Term id="pwm">PWM</Term>. 50% on means roughly half power; the motor's own inertia smooths the pulsing into steady spinning.

So the `speed` <Term id="variable">variable</Term> in your program becomes a duty-cycle percentage on real hardware. The block you've been dragging maps to a real, specific electrical behaviour.
